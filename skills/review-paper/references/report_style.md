# Report Style

Load this file when drafting the final referee report.

This file distills useful conventions from public review materials and referee-guideline documents. Use it to improve tone, structure, and selectivity, not to imitate another field's substantive standards.

## Core Style Principles

- Start with an accurate summary of the paper's contribution as the reviewer sees it.
- State what works before stating what blocks publication.
- Keep the list of essential points short.
- Separate publication-blocking concerns from optional suggestions.
- Write like a referee, not an anonymous coauthor.
- Be specific enough that the authors know what to fix, but do not turn the report into a rewrite plan for the whole paper.

## Structural Patterns Worth Reusing

### Pattern 1. Concise summary first

Useful public reports often begin by summarizing:

- the question
- the method or design
- the main finding
- the reviewer's overall view of the contribution

This helps the authors and editor see whether the reviewer understood the paper before reading the criticism.

### Pattern 2. Essential points are few

Strong referee guidance often separates:

- essential points: the few issues that determine publishability
- suggestions: everything else

Default rule: no more than 3 essential points. If you need more, the paper is usually not close enough to warrant a long revise-and-resubmit style roadmap.
Exception: if there are multiple distinct fatal flaws, separate them rather than compressing them into one vague omnibus point.

### Pattern 3. Strength plus block

The most useful overall-assessment paragraphs typically say:

- what is interesting, promising, or well executed
- what single issue or small set of issues prevents acceptance

This is better than a wall of criticism with no signal about what the paper is doing well.

### Pattern 4. Concrete, non-performative requests

Major comments should ask for things like:

- a specific falsification or placebo
- a sharper contribution claim
- a correction to interpretation
- a missing literature comparison
- a clearer explanation of institutional or data context

Avoid vague complaints such as "the paper needs more robustness" unless you name the threat and the check that would address it.

## Tone Guidance

- Professional, direct, and non-theatrical.
- Firm on serious flaws, but not insulting.
- Generous with justified praise.
- Do not perform omniscience. If something is uncertain, say so.
- Do not ask for an endless menu of extensions just because they would be interesting.
- Do not let the report sound like a polished AI synopsis. Prefer concrete referee language over generic evaluative prose.

## Concision Guidance

- A typical report should be short enough for an editor to scan quickly.
- If the paper is promising, spend your detail budget on the 1 to 3 issues most likely to change the editorial outcome.
- Minor comments should be genuinely minor.
- Brevity should not hide distinct fatal problems or the external evidence needed to justify them.

## Useful Writing Moves

- "The paper asks..., uses..., and finds..."
- "The central strength of the paper is..."
- "The main issue is not implementation but interpretation..."
- "The paper would be substantially stronger if..."
- "This is a suggestion rather than a publication-blocking concern..."
- "If the authors cannot provide this evidence, the claim should be narrowed..."

## Anti-AI Referee Guardrails

- Avoid empty praise such as `important topic`, `interesting paper`, or `valuable contribution` unless followed immediately by the specific reason.
- Avoid boilerplate criticism such as `the framing needs to be strengthened` or `the paper needs more robustness` unless the exact missing comparison, test, or interpretation is named.
- Avoid executive-summary cadence in which every sentence is equally polished and interchangeable.
- Avoid turning the summary paragraph into a generic abstract. It should identify the question, design, and finding in the reviewer's own concrete terms.
- Prefer a few precise paragraphs over a long sequence of near-identical bullets.
- Do not use AI-marker vocabulary: `delve`, `comprehensive`, `nuanced`, `multifaceted`, `pivotal`, `robust` (unless referring to statistical robustness), `innovative`, `landscape`, `underscore`, `showcase`, `leverage`, `utilize`. Use plain alternatives.
- Do not hedge every statement equally. Be assertive on clear methodological points; hedge only where the reviewer is genuinely uncertain.
- Avoid `furthermore`, `moreover`, `notably` as mechanical transitions. Let the logical flow carry the argument, or use plain connectors (`but`, `still`, `and yet`).
- Vary sentence length and paragraph structure. A report where every paragraph has three sentences of similar length reads as generated.
- Name specific tables, figures, page numbers, coefficients, and variable names. Concrete references to the manuscript are the strongest signal that a human actually read the paper.

## Anti-Patterns To Avoid

- Reviewer-as-coauthor:
  long wish lists, redesigning the paper around your own preferences, or demanding many side projects
- Hidden recommendation:
  listing many "minor" points that are actually fatal concerns
- Generic robustness rhetoric:
  asking for "more checks" without naming the relevant threat
- Summaryless criticism:
  launching into complaints without first showing you understood the paper
- Inflated certainty:
  asserting novelty or institutional facts without checking them
- Hidden sourcing:
  relying on outside checks for novelty or institutional context without showing the reader which external evidence mattered
- Generic polished prose:
  criticism or praise that could be pasted into many unrelated reports with almost no change
